Reading a simple weekly traffic scorecard

A weekly scorecard should support one or two useful decisions, not bury the reader in metrics. This guide shows how to connect traffic data with real outcomes.

Use a fixed reporting window

Choose the same seven-day period and timezone each week. A full Monday-to-Sunday week should not be compared casually with a six-day partial week. Record the date when the report was prepared, because analytics and affiliate networks can update their numbers later. Add short notes about major events: a new guide, a tracking change, a broken form, a paused advertisement, or a change in traffic source. Do not fill missing figures with invented estimates. If a platform's records are unavailable, mark them not available rather than zero. Zero means the event was measured and none occurred; missing data means you cannot make that claim. A consistent window makes your comparisons fairer even when traffic is small.

Keep the columns understandable

A basic table can contain source, landing page, tracked sessions, engaged sessions, confirmed email subscriptions, verified affiliate sales, advertising spend, and one observation. Keep hosting requests in a separate technical section if they matter for server troubleshooting. Do not mix ad clicks with website sessions or call every link click a lead. For educational pages, a visitor may find the answer without subscribing, so add a note about the page's intended reader outcome. Avoid collecting unnecessary personal information just to populate the report. You can make a useful decision from aggregated numbers. When a metric cannot be measured reliably, omit it or explain the limitation rather than creating a false sense of precision.

Calculate only meaningful rates

The observed signup rate can be calculated as confirmed subscriptions divided by tracked landing-page sessions, multiplied by 100. Advertising cost per signup is spend divided by confirmed signups, but if there are no signups, that rate is undefined. Do not invent a finite cost for an event that never happened. Verified revenue minus attributable campaign costs can describe a narrow campaign result, but full business profit also includes other expenses and time. Label currencies and denominators. Always show raw counts beside percentages. A change from one signup to two is a 100% increase, but it may still be far too little evidence to support a confident decision. Rates help summarize a report; they do not replace judgment.

Interpret changes without guessing causes

Suppose social referrals increased but email signups stayed flat. Investigate whether the new social post attracted the intended audience, whether the destination fulfilled its promise, and whether the form worked. Do not automatically conclude that social traffic is poor. If search traffic drops after an edit, check reporting and indexing before assuming the edit caused the decline. Write one hypothesis and one practical next test. For example, clarify the opening paragraph of a guide for beginners and review comparable traffic afterward. Record when the change occurred and avoid altering several variables simultaneously. If a paid source cannot demonstrate relevant visitors or the budget cap is reached, pause it instead of chasing bigger counters.

Worked example and decision

A hypothetical site records 300 tracked sessions, 120 engaged sessions, nine confirmed subscriptions, zero verified affiliate sales, and $12 CAD of advertising spend. Its observed signup rate is 9 ÷ 300 × 100 = 3%. The recorded advertising cost per signup is $12 ÷ 9, approximately $1.33 CAD, excluding other expenses. Because there are no verified sales, the report does not claim a positive return. The publisher notes that most signups came from one educational article and decides to improve related-reading links before buying more traffic. One week is not enough to establish causation; small samples and delayed attribution require caution.

Practical checklist

  1. Use a consistent seven-day window.
  2. Separate requests, sessions, leads, and sales.
  3. Mark missing figures distinctly from zero.
  4. Show raw counts and clear denominators.
  5. Write one evidence-based hypothesis.
  6. Choose one next action with a budget limit.
